The short story "Two Kinds" by Amy Tan is a narration from the perspective of Jing-mei, the first-generation American daughter of the family. It depicts the complicated dynamic between a girl who has never envisioned herself as a genius and a mother who only wishes the best for her daughter. Their mutual misunderstanding and conflicting views of success threaten to sever their bond. The mother Suyuan's tragic loss of her two baby daughters is ultimately a motivating factor to shape Jing-mei into the success of the family. For her, this is a second chance. Additionally, the lack of financial stability in her household suggests a desire for her child to never worry about money like her mother.

Having full belief in Jing-mei's potential, Suyuan is determined to push her as far as she can. So many different kinds of prodigies Suyuan tries to turn Jing-mei into, and yet, nothing works. In fact, Jing-mei grows this resolve to prove to her mother that she can never be such a prodigy. While preparing for a piano recital, for example, Jing-mei refuses to put any genuine effort into practicing the piano and decides to "put a stop to her foolish pride."

### My overall opinion
Jing-mei and Suyuan have different needs. Jing-mei needs a mother who can accept who she is—or that is what she thinks. Many might state that Jing-mei is simply lazy or indifferent. However, she deliberately does not work diligently towards her mother's goals because she misunderstands her intentions.
Striving to work harder does not inherently alter one's identity. However, since Jing-mei ultimately believes that her mother wants her to be a completely different person, obeying her mother's orders and constantly trying to meet her expectations means actively changing who she really is.
From a psychological standpoint, Jing-mei is trying to preserve her identity and protect herself.

Suyuan, however, is a different story. The constant pressure she burdens her daughter with comes from love. As someone who has experienced hardship after hardship, she wishes her child would have an easier life—a life that guarantees plentiful opportunities, comfort, and high status. All she asks of Jing-mei is to try her best. After all, she had gone through so much to provide everything she could for her daughter. I inferred that Suyuan would feel great irritation towards her daughter—not just because she isn't doing as she is told, but due to the sacrifices she has made for Jing-mei. Though I can see why Suyuan would call her daughter "ungrateful", doing so most likely caused great pain for Jing-mei. The story suggests that the girl feels a twinge of self-hatred for only being the ordinary girl that she sees in the mirror—not a prodigy, not the next Shirley Temple, nor a talented pianist. Hearing your mother call you ungrateful on top of that would surely be unpleasant.

Though the behaviors of both characters are understandable, that doesn't mean they should be excused. While Jing-mei may have resented her mother, the effort that went into not trying her best could have easily gone to actually working to do better. Slacking off on purpose to spite her mother was quite frustrating to read about.

Furthermore, Suyuan is very controlling as a parent, forcing her child to do what Suyuan thinks is best for her without considering what Jing-mei might want. Jing-mei probably believes that Suyuan is trying to make up for her own life by shaping Jing-mei's into one that fulfills what Suyuan's never did.

Undeniably, both characters could have aimed to empathize with each other more.

Suyuan dies when Jing-mei is in her 30s. Unfortunately, the ending where the two make up and resolve their misunderstanding never came.
I imagine that Suyuan passed away thinking that she failed as a mother and that Jing-mei faced her mother's death with the guilt of never having been the daughter Suyuan wanted her to be.
